Sandakphu Phalut Trek 
Walk the Singalila Ridge beneath the Sleeping Buddha 
From forest paths and prayer flags to open ridgelines, Sandakphu–Phalut brings the Eastern Himalaya into view 
one walking day at a time. On clear days, the panorama includes Everest, Kangchenjunga, Lhotse and Makalu—
four of the world’s five highest mountains. The Kangchenjunga group forms the celebrated Sleeping Buddha 
skyline, while the route passes through mountain settlements and the forests of Singalila National Park. 
Join BOOTpaths for six days of trekking, with an arrival-day transfer to Manebhanjang and a return to Siliguri. This 
is a rewarding, non-technical trek for people who prepare for long walks, steep climbs, sustained descents and 
basic mountain stays.

A ridge with a remarkable horizon 
Four Himalayan giants in one panorama 
Sandakphu is celebrated for its long Himalayan skyline. Everest, Lhotse and Makalu appear in the distant western 
panorama; Kangchenjunga commands the nearer view. Visibility depends on cloud, haze and weather, so 
mountain sightings are never guaranteed. 
Meet the Sleeping Buddha 
The outline of Kangchenjunga and its neighbouring peaks resembles a reclining figure. With your guide, follow 
the skyline and learn how people recognise the Sleeping Buddha. Dawn often offers a memorable opportunity to 
watch the snow peaks change colour. 
Go beyond Sandakphu to Phalut 
The extended ridge walk is what makes this journey more than a Sandakphu visit. The approximately 21 km stage 
to Phalut combines open country, repeated rises and falls, and long stretches exposed to wind. It is the itinerary’s 
main endurance day. 
Walk through a living mountain landscape 
The route changes from forest and bamboo to higher ridges and back into wooded valleys. Singalila protects red-
panda habitat and supports rich birdlife. Wildlife is elusive; quiet observation matters more than chasing a 
sighting. Rhododendron and magnolia flowers are seasonal, especially associated with spring. 
Experience village life with respect 
Manebhanjang, Tumling, Kalpokhri and the villages below Phalut bring local hospitality, simple meals, prayer flags 
and everyday mountain life into the journey. At Kalpokhri, respect the lake’s local religious significance. Ask 
before photographing residents, homes or religious interiors. 
Finish among forests and rivers 
After the high ridge, the descent towards Gorkhey offers a different rhythm: forest paths, small settlements and 
riverside scenery. The final walking stage continues towards Srikhola before the road journey back to Siliguri. 
Choose the season for the experience 
Spring brings flowering forests; autumn often favours clearer mountain views. Winter adds biting wind, freezing 
conditions and possible snow or ice, with greater disruption risk. Snowfall and clear skies cannot be promised. 
Singalila has a monsoon closure, generally 16 June–15 September; current opening orders must be checked 
before any departure.

Your journey from the plains to the ridge 
Day 0–Day 6 is seven calendar days and six overnight stays. Distances, elevations and times below are planning estimates, not a 
final vendor operating schedule. Walking times allow ordinary breaks but may increase with conditions.  
Day 0   Siliguri to Manebhanjang 
Travel day • Drive approximately 85–100 km / 4–5 hours • Overnight Manebhanjang, about 1,930 m  
Meet at the confirmed Siliguri pickup point (11 AM) and drive into the Darjeeling hills. Settle into the base stay, 
meet the local guide and complete the document and equipment checks. The evening briefing covers the route, 
weather, walking pace, health reporting and green-trail practices. Keep activity gentle and turn in early. 
Arrive in the region the previous evening where possible. If arriving on Day 0, use only a flight or train that 
reaches before the organiser’s confirmed pickup cutoff. Airport and NJP pickups must be agreed explicitly. 
Day 1   Manebhanjang to Tumling 
Approximately 11–13 km / 6–7 hours • Overnight Tumling, about 2,970 m  
Start early and climb towards Chitrey, continuing through the Meghma area to Tumling. This is a substantial first 
ascent, gaining roughly 1,000 m in sleeping elevation. Walk at a steady conversational pace and use short, regular 
breaks. Where access and time permit, the guide can introduce the monasteries and settlement history along the 
way. 
At Tumling, settle in and enjoy the skyline if the weather clears. Report headache, nausea, unusual tiredness or 
dizziness promptly; the next day’s plan depends on how the group is feeling. 
Day 2   Tumling to Kalpokhri 
Approximately 12–13 km / 6–7 hours • Overnight Kalpokhri, about 3,180 m  
After a possible sunrise view, follow the route through the Gairibas area and climb towards Kalpokhri. Forest 
sections, uneven paths and undulating terrain mean that distance alone does not describe the effort. Follow the 
guide through park and border checkpoints. 
Kalpokhri takes its name from its dark lake. Spend a quiet moment by the water, keeping away from sensitive 
edges and respecting local customs. Do not wash, throw offerings or discard anything into the lake. Warm up, eat 
well and prepare for the steeper climb to Sandakphu. 
Sandakphu sunrise and the long walk to Phalut 
Day 3   Kalpokhri to Sandakphu 
Approximately 6–8 km / 3–4 hours • Overnight Sandakphu, about 3,636 m  
Walk towards Bikheybhanjang before the steeper final ascent to Sandakphu, the highest point in West Bengal. 
Although this is a shorter distance, the climb is sustained and the altitude is noticeable. Keep the pace easy and 
avoid treating the final slope as a race. 
On arrival, settle into the accommodation, have a warm meal and rest. If conditions and your health permit, 
make a short, gentle visit to a nearby viewpoint. The mountain panorama may reveal itself in brief openings in 
the cloud; allow time to simply watch.

Prepare the evening before the Phalut stage: refill drinking water, pack lunch and trail snacks, check the 
headlamp, and keep wind and rain layers accessible. The guide should confirm trail conditions and the group’s 
readiness before proceeding. 
Day 4   Sandakphu to Phalut 
Approximately 21 km / 8–10 hours, sometimes longer • Overnight Phalut, about 3,600 m  
Begin early, with a brief sunrise view only if it fits the departure plan. Follow the long ridge towards Phalut, 
commonly passing the Sabargram area. Clear weather opens wide views towards the Everest group and 
Kangchenjunga. The terrain repeatedly rises and falls, so this is not a simple level walk between two similar 
elevations. 
This is the hardest day of the programme. Wind, cold, poor visibility and limited shelter can make progress 
slower. Keep water and food within reach, take planned breaks and stay with the group. Do not separate to 
photograph distant viewpoints or follow an alternative trail. 
Phalut accommodation is limited and basic. Arrive, change into dry layers, eat and rest. The next morning’s 
viewpoint visit remains subject to weather and how the group feels. 
A flexible plan is part of a good trek 
The guide must be able to shorten, reroute or stop the trek when weather, trail access or health makes that 
necessary. A split night on the ridge may be sensible for some groups, but changes the six-night plan unless 
another section is altered. It must be agreed in advance, not assumed to be included. 
Through Gorkhey to the journey home 
Day 5   Phalut to Gorkhey 
Approximately 14–15 km / 5–7 hours • Overnight Gorkhey, about 2,300–2,400 m  
If conditions allow, visit the Phalut viewpoint early before beginning the long descent. The trail drops from the 
high country into forest and bamboo, with a very different atmosphere from the exposed ridge. 
Take short, controlled steps on steep or slippery sections. Trekking poles can help with balance and reduce 
fatigue on the descent. Avoid racing downhill; knees and ankles still have another walking day ahead. 
Reach Gorkhey and settle into the village stay. Spend the afternoon resting or taking a gentle local walk with 
permission. Keep well away from unsafe riverbanks and do not enter fast or cold water. 
Day 6   Gorkhey to Srikhola and Siliguri 
Walking estimate 12–16 km / 5–7 hours • Then drive approximately 5–7 hours • Programme ends in Siliguri  
Leave early for the final walk, commonly through Samanden and the Rammam area towards Srikhola. The exact 
trail, village stops and usable vehicle pickup point must be confirmed by the local operator. Meet the transfer 
vehicle and continue to Siliguri. 
This is a long combined trekking and driving day. Allow approximately 10–14 hours or more from departure to 
Siliguri, depending on the confirmed trail, breaks, road conditions and traffic. An evening or late-evening arrival is 
possible. 
Plan an additional Siliguri hotel night and fly or take your onward train the following day. This extra stay is outside 
the six-night trek package unless separately included in your booking. Avoid a same-day flight booking based on 
an optimistic arrival estimate.

Mountain stays and meals that sustain the walk 
Simple rooms and warm hospitality 
Expect basic homestays, trekker huts or lodges according to confirmed availability. Rooms may be twin, triple or 
dormitory-style, with shared bathrooms and limited privacy. Do not assume attached toilets, room heating, 
reliable hot showers or hotel-standard linen. Phalut is especially basic and has limited capacity. 
Fresh food with local character 
Meals are generally simple and filling: rice, dal, vegetables, roti, porridge or other locally available breakfast 
dishes. Noodles, soup, eggs or regional dishes may be offered where supplies allow. Treat this as a typical menu, 
not a guarantee of individual dishes. Lunch may be packed on longer stages. 
Inform BOOTpaths about allergies, dietary restrictions and food preferences before booking. Remote kitchens 
may not be able to prevent all cross-contact. Vegetarian food is usually the easiest requirement to organise; 
vegan, gluten-free and other strict diets need specific confirmation. Carry a small reserve of suitable snacks. 
Water and everyday practicalities 
Carry reusable bottles with around 2 litres of capacity, adjusted to the day’s refill opportunities. Eat regularly and 
drink to thirst without forcing excessive water. 
Electricity, mobile reception and charging may be intermittent. Carry a power bank, keep batteries warm and tell 
family that daily calls may not be possible. Some stays may charge for power or hot water. Carry small-
denomination cash; do not depend on UPI or ATMs after the base village. 
Altitude awareness belongs in every briefing 
Fitness does not prevent altitude illness. Report headache with nausea, dizziness or unusual fatigue immediately; 
do not ascend with symptoms. Worsening symptoms require descent. Breathlessness at rest, confusion or 
difficulty walking straight require urgent descent and medical help. Never leave an unwell trekker alone. 
Tell the trek leader about health conditions, allergies and medicines before travel. Seek individual medical advice 
when appropriate. Acetazolamide or other medication decisions belong with a clinician; informing a trek leader 
does not replace a prescription. A first-aid kit does not imply a doctor, oxygen supply or so.

Pack light and be ready for changing weather 
Carry on every departure 
•  Documents: original accepted photo ID, copies, emergency contacts, insurance details if purchased, personal 
prescriptions and any required medical declaration. Confirm border-related document requirements for your 
nationality. 
•  Backpack: a comfortable 50–60 litre pack if carrying all gear, or a 20–25 litre daypack only when offloading is 
confirmed. Use a rain cover plus waterproof inner bags. 
•  Footwear: broken-in trekking shoes with a grippy sole, 3–4 pairs of trekking socks and light footwear for the 
stay. Check fit on descents before the trip. 
•  Clothing: 2–3 quick-drying tops, 2 trekking trousers, underwear and a dry set for sleeping. Avoid jeans and 
heavy cotton for walking. 
•  Warm layers: a fleece or warm mid-layer, insulated jacket, thermal layer, warm cap, light gloves and neck 
covering. Cold-weather protection is needed even outside winter. 
•  Weather protection: a hooded waterproof shell and rain trousers or suitable poncho; secure loose fabric in 
wind. Pack a sun hat, UV-protective sunglasses, sunscreen and lip balm. 
•  Walking essentials: headlamp with spare batteries, trekking poles, reusable water bottles, personal trail snacks 
and a small whistle. 
•  Health and hygiene: regular medicines with a small reserve, personal blister care, toothbrush, small towel, 
sanitiser, toilet paper and menstrual supplies as needed. 
•  Practical extras: power bank, charging cable, small-denomination cash, reusable waste bags and sealable bags 
for used hygiene products. 
Keep these in your daypack 
Water, snacks or packed lunch, rain and a jacket, headlamp, personal medicines, ID, valuables and a small waste 
bag must remain with you. An offloaded bag may not be accessible during the day. 
Additional items to carry for winter treks 
•  A warmer insulated jacket; extra thermal top and bottom; thick dry sleeping socks; insulated waterproof gloves 
with liners; a warmer balaclava or neck gaiter. 
•  Water-resistant or waterproof trekking footwear suitable for cold conditions, spare dry socks and gaiters when 
snow conditions warrant them. 
•  Traction devices such as microspikes only when recommended by the leader for the actual trail; fit and practise 
with them in advance. They do not make every icy slope safe. 
•  A winter-rated sleeping bag if the accommodation plan requires it; agree the comfort rating with the operator 
rather than relying on an advertised extreme rating. 
•  An insulated bottle or bottle sleeve, additional battery protection and optional hand warmers. Confirm snow 
and ice conditions before final packing.

A weekly rhythm that leaves room to recover 
Keep at least one full rest day each week. Two 20–30 minute strength sessions can include step-ups, squats, split 
squats, calf raises and core work, using good form and comfortable loads. Build hills and time on your feet before 
adding speed. Training in flat terrain should include stairs and deliberate downhill practice where safe. 
Prepare your mindset as well as your legs 
Practise a sustainable pace and accept that a group trek involves waiting, shared rooms, early starts and changing 
plans. Break a long day into the next rest stop or landmark. Ask for help early, tell the truth about symptoms and 
treat turnaround decisions as sound mountain judgement. 
Before departure, aim to finish a 5–6 hour practice walk with your intended pack and still manage an easy walk 
the next day. This is a useful readiness check, not a guarantee that the 8–10 hour Phalut day will feel easy. 
Discuss any concerns with the organiser early.

Leave a lighter footprint on Singalila 
Carry your waste all the way out 
Bring reusable bottles and reduce disposable packaging before departure. Carry back wrappers, tissues, cigarette 
ends, sanitary waste and food scraps, including peels. A lodge bin is not proof that waste will be managed safely; 
follow the organiser’s agreed waste-return system. 
Give wildlife space and the forest quiet 
Stay on established trails. Do not pick flowers, collect plants, feed animals or approach wildlife for photographs. 
Avoid loud music, bird-call playback and flash around animals. Keep exact locations of sensitive wildlife sightings 
private. A red-panda encounter must never become a chase. 
Protect water and use facilities responsibly 
Use designated toilets whenever available. Keep soap, detergents, toothpaste and washing away from streams 
and lakes, even when labelled biodegradable. Ask the guide for the approved procedure where toilets are 
unavailable. Use water sparingly in mountain stays. 
Respect homes and sacred places 
Ask permission before taking or publishing identifiable photographs of residents, participants or children. 
Remove footwear where requested and follow local guidance at monasteries, shrines and prayer structures. Do 
not step over religious objects or disturb the Kalpokhri lake. Avoid photographing border or security 
infrastructure without permission. 
Support the people who make the trek possible 
Choose local products and services, pay agreed prices and avoid wasteful food orders. Treat guides, hosts, drivers 
and porters with dignity. Any porter or animal support should use agreed safe loads, proper equipment, fair 
payment and appropriate rest. Do not give sweets or money to children; ask about responsible community 
support instead. 
Prevent fire and follow access rules 
No litter burning or casual bonfires. Do not collect firewood. Follow park, border and guide instructions, including 
any seasonal or weather-related restrictions. Drone use is not assumed permitted; protected-area and border 
restrictions require explicit verification and permissions. 
No one left behind policy by BOOTpaths 
Follow the lead and sweep arrangements, regroup at junctions and inform the team before stopping. Give way 
safely on narrow paths and keep a clear distance from pack animals. Do not split off for a shortcut, photo or 
faster finish. Report unsafe behaviour without turning it into a confrontation. 
BOOTpaths encourages a quiet, alcohol-free trek, responsible photography and care for both the landscape and 
the people who live here. Every Step, A New Story.

Your questions answered about the trek 
01   What makes Sandakphu–Phalut special? 
The route combines a famous Himalayan panorama with an extended ridge walk, mountain villages and forest 
descents. Phalut adds a substantial walking challenge beyond a Sandakphu-only visit. 
02   Can we see Everest from Sandakphu? 
On clear days, Everest can be seen in the distant western mountain group with Lhotse and Makalu. Clouds and haze 
can hide the view completely. 
03   What is the Sleeping Buddha? 
It is the reclining-figure outline associated with Kangchenjunga and its neighbouring peaks. Your guide can help you 
identify the skyline when visibility permits. 
04   Is this trek easy or moderate? 
For this full itinerary, moderate is the more useful description. The first climb, approximately 21 km Phalut stage, long 
descent and consecutive walking days demand preparation. 
05   Can a first-time Himalayan trekker join? 
A well-prepared beginner may be suitable after discussing fitness and health with the organiser. Previous short hikes 
and back-to-back walking practice will help. 
06   Do I need climbing skills or ropes? 
The usual route is non-technical. Snow, ice or severe weather can still make sections unsafe and may require 
equipment changes, rerouting or cancellation. 
07   Is there an age limit? 
The organiser must set the participation policy. Suitability depends on health, fitness and supervision; do not describe 
this itinerary as suitable for every age without assessment. 
08   How high do we go? 
The highest planned point is Sandakphu, approximately 3,636 m. Accommodation elevations and published readings 
vary slightly; Phalut is around 3,600 m. 
09   How far do we walk in total? 
Budget approximately 80–85 km for the proposed complete walking route. The total will be revised after the vendor 
confirms trail choices and the final pickup point. 
10   How long is the longest walking day? 
Sandakphu to Phalut is approximately 21 km. Allow roughly 8–10 hours, sometimes longer, including breaks and 
depending on weather and group pace. 
11   Why does the programme begin with Day 0? 
Day 0 is the arrival and transfer day, including the Manebhanjang overnight stay. Days 0–6 total seven calendar days; it 
is not an extra day outside the package. 
12   How many nights are included? 
The revised plan contains six: Manebhanjang, Tumling, Kalpokhri, Sandakphu, Phalut and Gorkhey. The vendor must 
confirm and price all six. 
13   Is Srikhola an overnight stop? 
Not in this seven-day plan. We walk to the confirmed Srikhola roadhead and transfer to Siliguri on Day 6. 
14   Can the long Phalut stage be split? 
Potentially, if authorised accommodation and logistics allow. An extra ridge night changes the programme unless 
another stage is redesigned; it needs a separate agreement. 
15   Are all route spellings the same places? 
Common variants include Manebhanjang/Maneybhanjyang, Kalpokhri/Kalipokhri, Gorkhey/Ghorkey and 
Srikhola/Shrikola. Match bookings to the actual property and pickup location.

Do not assume so. Shared toilets are common in basic trekking accommodation. Confirm property-specific 
arrangements and any accessibility concerns before booking. 
34   Will there be hot showers and heating? 
These are not guaranteed. Hot water may be limited or paid, and rooms may be unheated. Bring adequate warm 
layers and follow the host’s heating-safety instructions. 
35   What food will be served? 
Expect simple local meals suited to trekking. The exact menu depends on supplies and kitchens. Long walking days may 
require packed lunches. 
36   Can vegetarian or special diets be arranged? 
Vegetarian meals are generally practical, but all requirements need notice. Strict allergy, vegan or gluten-free needs 
require direct confirmation of ingredients and kitchen limitations. 
37   Is drinking water provided? 
The final package must specify treated-water provision and charges. Carry reusable bottles and ask about refills before 
each day; untreated streams are not a safe default. 
38   Will I get mobile reception and Wi-Fi? 
Coverage is patchy and Wi-Fi cannot be relied on. Near the border, watch for unintended roaming. Give family a 
realistic communication plan before leaving. 
39   Can I charge my phone or camera? 
Some properties offer limited charging, sometimes at extra cost. Bring a power bank and protect batteries from cold; 
do not rely on a socket every night. 
40   Can I pay digitally on the trail? 
Sometimes, but network and electricity failures can interrupt payment. Carry enough small-denomination cash for 
agreed extras and keep it secure. 
41   Can I offload my backpack? 
Only if porter or animal support is arranged. Confirm availability, charges, weight limits, route coverage and suitable 
working conditions. Carry all daytime essentials yourself. 
42   What shoes should I wear? 
Well-fitting, broken-in trekking shoes with reliable grip. Test them on descents with your trekking socks. Bring more 
weather-resistant footwear if winter conditions require it. 
43   Do I need a sleeping bag? 
That depends on the confirmed bedding and accommodation plan. Ask before packing or renting, especially for Phalut 
and winter departures. 
44   Are trekking poles useful? 
Yes, especially on the long descent and uneven ground. Practise using them beforehand and stow them safely in 
crowded rooms and vehicles. 
45   What should I carry for menstrual care? 
Bring the products you normally use, spare underwear, sanitiser and sealable disposal bags. Water and privacy can be 
limited; carry used products out for proper disposal.

46   Will we definitely see snow? 
No. Snow depends on recent weather, season and elevation. Winter may bring bare, icy ground as well as snowfall;

47   When is the park closed? 
The usual monsoon closure is 16 June–15 September. The operator must check current park orders, temporary 
restrictions and the opening status for your exact dates. 
48   Will we see a red panda? 
Possibly, but sightings are rare and never guaranteed. Keep quiet, remain on the trail and follow the guide’s wildlife-
distance instructions. 
49   How fit should I be? 
Prepare for repeated long walks with hills and your planned pack. Use the eight-week plan and discuss difficulty 
completing practice outings with the organiser. 
50   Can I get altitude sickness even if I am fit? 
Yes. Fitness is not protection against altitude illness. Follow the reporting and ascent guidance in the health section. [8] 
51   What if I have headache or nausea? 
Tell the guide immediately and do not continue ascending while symptomatic. Worsening symptoms or serious 
warning signs require descent and medical help. 
52   Should I take Diamox preventively? 
Discuss acetazolamide with a qualified clinician before travel. Do not use another trekker’s prescription or treat 
medication as a substitute for an appropriate ascent plan.  
53   Can I join with a medical condition? 
Disclose relevant conditions, allergies and regular medicines. Seek individual clinical advice and confirm that the trek’s 
remoteness and support arrangements are appropriate for you.  
54   Is a doctor or oxygen cylinder included? 
The vendor lists a guide’s first-aid kit only. Doctor support, oxygen, trained staffing and emergency equipment must be 
separately confirmed before they are advertised. 
55   How does evacuation work? 
The guide coordinates the safest available route and transport. Weather, communication and road access can delay 
help; there is no assumed helicopter or immediate vehicle rescue. 
56   Do I need insurance? 
Trek insurance is included in this cost. BOOTpaths gives safety the first priority and hence the insurance made 
compulsory.  
57   What happens in severe weather? 
The route, timings or overnight stops may change, or the trek may end early. Participant safety and legal access take 
priority over completing the advertised route. 
58   Can I use a drone or play music? 
Do not assume drone permission in this protected border landscape. Avoid loud music; keep the experience quiet for 
wildlife, residents and fellow trekkers. 
59   What are the cancellation and early-exit rules? 
See BOOTpaths’ policy covering cancellation, replacement, weather changes, missed transfers, unused services and 
extra costs before payment.
